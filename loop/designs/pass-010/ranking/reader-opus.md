Ranking: B, C, A, D

## 1. B — wick

The broken law is drawn on the inhabitants themselves: candles above the floor are pale, legless and unlit, and the crisp, burning twins below them walk on little black shoes. So the joke is visible in every frame near the lens and does not depend on a single setpiece. It is also the most clearly separate from the existing four. The palette (milk white, soot black, one vermilion) has nothing in common with teal, coral, pastel or indigo, and its thumbnail (white over black, one red stroke with a spark) will read even as a stamp inside the indigo moon gate. It makes the best use of the climb of any proposal: the wheels of dancers become a five-petal figure seen from above as the camera rises round the chandelier, so the full turn turns a crowd into a pattern. The exit is the best "too thin" object of the four: the only lit flame in a room hours wide. The biggest risk is that the swap reads as an ordinary mirror floor, because the fresnel floor goes white at grazing angles, which is where a forward-looking camera mostly sees it. The legs have to be legible at t = 5 to 8, and white-on-white with no shadows can go flat at t = 16 to 20.

## 2. C — roost

The strongest idea about structure: the climbing turn carries the camera through a hole in a floor that two worlds share, so the climb is the hinge of the scene, not decoration. The lamp you flew under turns out to be the underside of a nest. A porcelain hen-dish that rattles her lid twice and floats up is warm and genuinely funny, and the exit is set up for fifteen seconds as a shut bowl that plainly cannot hold a stair shaft. The thumbnail (white ceiling, gold seam, dark below, a glowing half-disc) is clean. It ranks below B on difference and risk. The hanging, upside-down first half shares ground with orbit's "the street is overhead". The porcelain and celadon palette overlaps with A. And everything rides on a procedurally modelled hen being charming rather than lumpy, which is the hardest single modelling job among the four. The geometry is also tight: the slab crossing, and a slit portal that only fills the screen at 24.4.

## 3. A — slurp

The crazy straw is a lovely way to explain the rail: the camera's curve is an object everyone recognises. A sleeper blowing a soap bubble that is the next world, swelling in time with its breath, is the most charming exit punchline of the four. Rising tea, a lit floor and hoppers flipping mid-leap give plenty of motion. Its biggest weakness is the premise. A tea tray with a teapot and cups is the very first picture the other proposals struck. "Down is whatever is nearest" is spread across curls, up-pours and menisci, so it risks reading as a cluttered tea set rather than a law. The glowing straw under the whole track repeats burrow's glowing guide line. Light from below on furry faces can turn eerie. A squat pot inside the loop still has to avoid reading as the helix's column. Buildable, but the busiest set and the thinnest strangeness.

## 4. D — earshot

"Loud is near" is a clever law, but in a silent screensaver the viewer cannot hear the loudness. What remains on screen is party-horns that rush toward the camera and back, which the proposal itself admits may read as "creatures greeting me". The pressed-leaf pocket with stitches on the horizon is beautiful and its high-key green is truly new. But the coiled stalk on the helix axis, with the camera winding round it, is the helix's column again. The humming-hair exit is conceptually the thinnest of all, yet a blurred vesica built from eleven ghost strands, with no temporal anti-aliasing at 4K, is the most likely to look like shimmer or a glitch. A portal that exists only while it vibrates is also hard to frame as a crafted object. Highest risk, least certain to read.

## Borrowing

Into wick, I would borrow roost's single environment function: a curtained horizon with a seam of gold light, sampled by every glazed surface as a fresnel reflection. Wick's biggest visual risk is white wax on a white room with no shadows going flat at t = 16 to 20. A bright horizon band reflected as a long highlight down every candle shaft would give each upper-world taper a crisp edge and a sense of volume for almost no cost. It would also tie the room together without breaking the no-shadow rule. A close second is slurp's breath clock. One slow 3.5 s cycle could drive the exit flame's swell, the lean of the wallflowers and the wheels' bob, which would give the last five seconds a single hypnotic rhythm.
